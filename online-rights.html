The Online Safety Act 2023

This landmark law came into force in 2024 and changed what social media platforms must do to protect UK users. Under the Act, platforms must:

  • Remove illegal content quickly — including hateful, fraudulent, or violent content
  • Enforce their own community guidelines consistently
  • Provide clear, accessible appeals processes when they remove content or suspend accounts
  • Offer extra protections for children under 18
  • Be transparent about how their algorithms work
Ofcom enforces the Online Safety Act. If a platform repeatedly fails to comply, Ofcom can fine them up to 10% of their global annual turnover — or block access to the platform in the UK entirely.

Content removal & appeals

If a platform removes your post, video, or account, you now have the right to a clear explanation and a fair appeals process. This applies to Instagram, TikTok, YouTube, X, Facebook, and all major platforms operating in the UK.

  1. Read the reason given

    Platforms must explain which rule your content allegedly violated. No reason given? That's itself a breach of the Online Safety Act.

  2. Use the in-app appeal

    All major platforms have an appeal button. Use it and provide context — especially if the removal was an automated error.

  3. Request a human review

    If the first appeal fails, explicitly request a human moderator. Platforms must have this option under the Act.

  4. Complain to Ofcom

    If the platform fails to follow a proper process, report it at ofcom.org.uk. The platform risks serious fines.

Your GDPR data rights

UK GDPR gives you powerful rights over your personal data — not just with social media platforms, but with shops, employers, banks, and any company that holds information about you.

📋

Right of access

Request a copy of all personal data a company holds on you (a Subject Access Request).

✏️

Right to rectification

Ask them to correct inaccurate or incomplete data.

🗑️

Right to erasure

Ask them to delete your data — the "right to be forgotten".

⏸️

Right to restrict

Ask them to stop processing your data while a dispute is resolved.

📦

Right to portability

Request your data in a machine-readable format.

🚫

Right to object

Object to your data being used for direct marketing — they must stop immediately.

Subject access requests (SARs)

A Subject Access Request (SAR) forces any company to show you all personal data they hold about you. Companies must respond within one calendar month, completely free.

You don't need a special form. Email their Data Protection Officer and write:

"I am writing to make a Subject Access Request under Article 15 of UK GDPR. Please provide all personal data you hold about me, including the purposes for which it is processed, who you share it with, and your retention period."
If they don't respond: Complain to the ICO at ico.org.uk — it's free and they have real enforcement powers.

Right to erasure — getting your data deleted

You can ask any company to delete all personal data they hold about you. They must comply if the data is no longer needed, you withdraw consent, or you object to processing and there's no overriding legitimate interest.

Erasure isn't always possible. Companies can refuse if they need to keep the data to comply with a legal obligation (e.g. tax records). They must explain their reason if they refuse.

Social media harassment

Online harassment is a criminal offence under multiple UK laws including the Online Safety Act 2023, Communications Act 2003, and Protection from Harassment Act 1997. It is illegal to:

  • Send messages that are grossly offensive or threatening
  • Persistently contact someone in a way that causes distress
  • Publish private images without consent (revenge porn)
  • Incite others to harass someone

What to do

  1. Screenshot everything with timestamps before blocking or reporting
  2. Report to the platform — they must act under the Online Safety Act
  3. Report to the police — call 101 (non-emergency) or 999 if you feel at immediate risk

Defamation online

Defamation is publishing a false statement that damages someone's reputation, covered by the Defamation Act 2013. For a statement to be defamatory it must be false, published to at least one other person, and have caused (or be likely to cause) serious harm to your reputation.

Defamation cases are expensive. They're heard in the High Court and can cost tens of thousands of pounds. Always seek advice from a media law solicitor before taking action.

Fake accounts impersonating you

Impersonation is illegal under the Fraud Act 2006 and the Online Safety Act. What to do:

  1. Report to the platform immediately — all major platforms have impersonation report options and must act quickly
  2. Report to Action Fraud — if money is being collected in your name
  3. Report to the police — if this is part of stalking or a harassment campaign
Tip: When reporting, include a link to your legitimate profile. This helps the platform confirm who you are and speeds up their review.
